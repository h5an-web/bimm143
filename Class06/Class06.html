Background

All functions in R have at least 3 things:

  • a name (we pick that and use it to call the function)
  • input arguments (one or more comma seperated inputs that go inside the brackets when we call the function)
  • the body (the lines of R code that do the work of the function)

5a. My randomly generated peptides start to look “unique in nature” at 8 amino acids. The 6 and 7 aa peptides had hits with 100% identity and 100% coverage, but from 8 aa onward no hit reached both.

5b. The number of possible peptides grows exponentially as 20^L, from about 6.4 × 10^7 possible 6-mers to about 8 × 10^16 possible 13-mers. The nr database contains hundreds of millions of proteins, each a few hundred amino acids long, which is far more than the number of possible 6’mers but far fewer than the number of possible longer peptides. So very short random peptides almost always appear somewhere in nr by chance, while longer ones quickly outgrow the known protein universe and are unlikely to have an exact match.

Connecting your findings to immunology (MHC class II and T-cell activation)

Q6. In 3–6 sentences total and using your Q5 data and the reasoning from Q5b, what do you think this minimum length is and why might it be a bad design choice for the immune system to present very short peptides?

Based on my Q5 data, I think the minimum length is around 9 to 13 amino acids. My random peptides of 6 and 7 aa had exact matches in nr, while from 8 aa onward none did, and by 13 aa the best hit covered only 69% of the sequence. This matches the biology, since MHC class II molecules bind peptides of about 13 to 25 aa, with a core of about 9 aa sitting in the binding groove. Presenting very short peptides would be a bad design choice because, as shown in Q5b, the sequence space for short lengths (20^6 ≈ 6.4 × 10^7) is small enough that almost any short peptide from a pathogen would also occur somewhere in the host’s own proteins. T cells would then be unable to tell self from non-self, so they would either attack the body’s own cells (autoimmunity) or, if those T cells were removed to prevent this, fail to respond to the pathogen. Longer peptides are almost always unique to the protein they came from, so they give T cells a reliable signal of what is foreign.
